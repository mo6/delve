# How do you know she is a witch?

Bedevere holds a stick like a pointer and a pointed hat like evidence. The
crowd behind him is already chanting. He does not join in.

"They bring me a woman," he says, "and they say she is a witch. I ask the only
question worth asking: *how do you know?* And they answer: she looks like one."

He puts the hat on a stool. "So I ask whether they dressed her up like that
themselves. They did. They even admit it, once you make them. The nose, the hat,
the whole costume: props. And then they offer the next piece of 'proof': she
turned someone into a newt."

"I asked the newt. The newt got better."

He taps the stick on the floor. "What you are watching is not investigation. It
is a crowd that decided first and is shopping for reasons afterward. 'She looks
like one' is not evidence; it is the costume they put on her. 'She turned him
into a newt' collapses the moment the victim walks in looking like himself."

> When the proof is the costume you put on the accused, you have not proved
> anything. You have staged a verdict.

"Your job in this room is not to save the woman. The film already decided how
that ends. Your job is to notice the shape of the argument: conclusion first,
reasons second, and any inconvenient fact politely ignored."

## Questions

### A crowd says a woman is a witch because she looks like one. What is the strongest problem with that claim?

- [ ] Pointed hats were never associated with witchcraft
- [x] Looking the part is not evidence when the crowd dressed her that way
- [ ] Witches never wear hats in folklore
- [ ] Crowds are always wrong about individuals

> The hat and nose are props the villagers supplied. Treating their own costume
> as proof is circular: they made her look like a witch, then cited the look.
>
> Crowds are not always wrong, and folklore does associate witches with pointed
> hats. Neither fact rescues an argument that cites a disguise the accusers put
> on.

### "She turned me into a newt" is strong evidence of witchcraft, provided the speaker is sincere.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> Sincerity is not the same as accuracy. The claim fails the moment the speaker
> is standing there as a human, not a newt. "I got better" is funny because it
> quietly deletes the only fact that would have mattered.

### Bedevere asks how they know she is a witch before he asks what should be done with her. Why does that order matter?

- [ ] Medieval trials always began with a method question
- [ ] It wastes time and lets the witch escape
- [x] It refuses to treat the verdict as already settled
- [ ] It flatters the crowd so they will listen

> Starting with "how do you know?" forces the crowd to show their working. If
> you start with the punishment, the verdict has already been smuggled in.
>
> It is not a period-procedure rule, and it is not flattery. It is the only
> move that keeps the conclusion from arriving dressed as a question.

### The villagers admit they dressed the woman as a witch, then keep treating the costume as proof. This is best described as:

- [ ] A careful experiment
- [x] Circular reasoning dressed up as observation
- [ ] A coincidence that happens to be true
- [ ] Peer review by the village

> They created the appearance, then cited the appearance. That loop is the
> whole joke, and the whole lesson. Calling it peer review or an experiment
> just gives the loop a respectable name.
