# What also floats?

Duckworth has a duck under one arm and a scrap of wood under the other. He
looks pleased with both, which should worry you.

"Bedevere taught them a method," he says. "Witches burn. Wood burns. Therefore
witches are made of wood. Wood floats. What else floats? A duck. Therefore, if
she weighs the same as a duck, she is made of wood, and therefore…"

He pauses for the punchline the crowd already wants.

"…a witch."

He sets the duck down gently. "Notice what happened. Each step sounds almost
reasonable if you take it alone. Wood burns; so do people on a pyre. Wood
floats; so do ducks. But the chain is a false analogy glued to a non sequitur.
Burning is not a property that proves composition. Floating is not a property
that proves witchcraft. And 'weighs the same as a duck' does not follow from
either."

"Also," he adds, "very small rocks, gravy, churches, and a duck were all
offered as things that float. The crowd was not doing science. It was free-
associating until someone said the word that let them light the fire."

> A chain of almost-plausible steps can still add up to nonsense. Check whether
> each link actually forces the next.

"The duck is adorable. The logic is not. Your examination is about spotting
where the analogy snaps."

## Questions

### In Bedevere's chain, "witches burn, wood burns, therefore witches are made of wood," what is the main flaw?

- [ ] Wood does not actually burn
- [x] Sharing one property does not prove two things are the same stuff
- [ ] Witches never burn in folklore
- [ ] The crowd should have used a larger duck

> Lots of unrelated things burn. Sharing "burns" does not make a person wooden,
> any more than sharing "floats" makes a duck a church. The analogy grabs one
> property and pretends it is identity.
>
> The duck's size is irrelevant, and the folklore point is a distraction.

### "What also floats? A duck." is a useful scientific control for proving witchcraft.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> A control would hold variables steady and test one claim. Naming a duck is a
> punchline that lets the crowd skip from floating to guilt. It is rhetoric, not
> method.

### Which of these is the best description of the villagers shouting answers like "bread," "apples," "very small rocks," and "a duck"?

- [ ] Peer-reviewed natural philosophy
- [ ] A complete list of buoyant materials
- [x] Free association until a useful word appears
- [ ] Careful measurement of density

> They are brainstorming punchlines, not measuring density. The moment someone
> says "duck," the argument can lurch to the weighing, which is what they
> wanted all along.

### If two objects weigh the same, what does that prove about whether one of them is a witch?

- [ ] They are made of the same material
- [ ] The lighter one is innocent
- [x] Almost nothing about witchcraft
- [ ] The heavier one sinks, so it cannot be wood

> Equal weight is equal weight. It does not identify composition, guilt, or
> buoyancy by itself. The sketch treats the scale as a moral instrument; it is
> only a scale.
