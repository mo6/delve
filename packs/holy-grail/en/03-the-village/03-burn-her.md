# And therefore… a witch

The Verdict does not smile. They have a set of scales, a duck, and the patience
of someone who has watched this joke land a thousand times.

"They weigh her against the duck," they say. "The scales balance. The crowd
cheers. Burn her. Curtain."

"What did the weighing actually show? That, on that day, with that duck, the
masses matched closely enough for comedy. What did the crowd *hear*? Proof. The
method was theatre that borrowed the costume of reason: questions, analogies,
a measurement. It felt like thinking. It was a ritual with props."

They tip the empty scale. "This is the danger outside the film, too. A process
can look careful (questions asked, comparisons made, numbers written down) and
still be built to reach a conclusion that was never in doubt. The villagers
did not discover she was a witch. They manufactured a chain that could only
end one way."

> Looking rigorous is not the same as being rigorous. Ask what outcome the
> method was allowed to produce.

"You may enjoy the scene. You should still refuse to confuse a punchline with
a proof. That is the door."

## Questions

### The woman weighs the same as a duck, so the crowd concludes she is a witch. What did the weighing actually establish?

- [x] That her mass matched the duck's closely enough for the gag
- [ ] That she was composed of wood
- [ ] That ducks are magical
- [ ] That the scales were calibrated by the king

> The scale reports a mass comparison. Everything else (wood, witchcraft,
> justice) is narration the crowd layered on top. The joke works because they
> treat a coincidence of weight as a moral verdict.

### A procedure that asks questions, makes comparisons, and uses a measuring tool is automatically fair.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> Tools and questions can decorate a fixed conclusion. Fairness depends on
> whether contrary evidence could have changed the outcome. Here it could not:
> the crowd wanted a burning.

### Which question best tests whether a "logical" trial is actually open to being wrong?

- [ ] How loud was the crowd?
- [ ] How expensive were the props?
- [x] What result would have cleared the accused?
- [ ] How many steps were in the argument?

> If no possible result clears her, the method is a costume. Step count, noise,
> and budget do not fix a closed conclusion.

### The funniest part of the witch scene is also the most useful warning for real arguments. What is that warning?

- [ ] Never trust ducks
- [ ] Never use scales outdoors
- [x] A confident chain of reasons can still be nonsense end to end
- [ ] Village life is inherently unjust

> The sketch is a masterclass in sounding logical while leaping gaps. The
> warning is about the chain, not about poultry or outdoor equipment.
