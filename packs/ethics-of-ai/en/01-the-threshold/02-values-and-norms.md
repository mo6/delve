# Values and norms

Soren draws a line in the dust with a stick. On one side he writes "value". On the other, "norm". "Confuse these," he says, "and every AI guideline you read afterward will sound like nonsense."

"A value," he says, "is simply how important something is to you. Some values are intrinsic: you want them for their own sake. Wellbeing. Not being deceived. Others are extrinsic, or instrumental: you want them because they get you something else. Money is the cleanest example. Nobody wants money the way they want to be happy; they want what it buys. Mix the two up and you end up optimizing a system for money when what you actually meant was wellbeing, and money was only ever the means."

"A norm," he continues, "is a value with a command attached. It says: because we hold this important, do this, or do not do that. Norms come in more flavors than people expect. Statistical norms are just what most people do, no judgment implied. Social norms are what your group expects of you, enforced by disapproval rather than law. Moral norms claim something stronger: this is right or wrong, regardless of who is watching. Legal norms are written down and backed by the state."

He taps the dust twice. "Here is the trap that catches almost everyone: legal does not mean moral, and moral does not mean legal. A law can be unjust; obeying it does not make you good. Something can be perfectly legal and still be wrong, and a great deal of what you will examine in this dungeon, a biased model that violates no statute, a design that exploits a loophole, lives exactly in that gap."

"One more distinction, small but sharp. A norm can be prescriptive, telling you what to do, or proscriptive, telling you what not to do. 'Explain your model's decisions' is prescriptive. 'Do not deploy a system you cannot explain' is proscriptive. Most AI guidelines mix both freely, and neither is weaker than the other."

"So when you read an AI ethics guideline, a set of principles some company published, remember what it actually is. It is not a value; nobody can command you to hold something important, that has to come from you. A guideline is a norm: a value someone judged important enough to turn into an instruction. Ask what value sits underneath it before you decide whether to follow it."

> A guideline is a norm built on a value; find the value before you obey the instruction.

## Questions

### A company's AI ethics guideline states: "Do not deploy a facial recognition system without an appeals process." What is this, in Soren's vocabulary?

- [x] A norm, specifically proscriptive, built on some underlying value
- [ ] A value in its own right
- [ ] A statistical norm
- [ ] A statement with no value behind it, since it only restricts behaviour

> It's a command, so it's a norm, and it forbids rather than requires, so it's proscriptive. It is tempting to call a purely restrictive sentence "value-neutral", but every norm rests on a value; here it's likely fairness or accountability for whoever the system misidentifies.

### If an AI practice is legal in a given country, it is therefore morally acceptable.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> Legal and moral are independent axes. Laws can lag behind a technology or actively permit a harmful practice; being lawful only tells you a legislature hasn't forbidden it, not that it's right.

### A company pursues AI investment purely to raise its stock price, treating the technology itself as worthless beyond that. Money, in this case, is functioning as...

- [x] An extrinsic (instrumental) value, wanted only for what it buys
- [ ] An intrinsic value, wanted for its own sake
- [ ] A moral norm
- [ ] A statistical norm

> Money is the textbook instrumental value: nobody wants the stock price for itself, they want what a higher price gets them. Calling it intrinsic mistakes the means for the end, which is exactly the mix-up Soren warns against.

### Every norm, once you trace it back far enough, rests on some value someone judged important.

- [x] True
- [ ] False

> That's Soren's core claim. Even a norm that reads as purely procedural, a form to fill in, a process to follow, traces back to an underlying value like safety, fairness, or honesty; it wasn't written for no reason.
