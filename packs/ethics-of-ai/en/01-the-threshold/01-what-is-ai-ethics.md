# What is AI ethics?

Helmi does not look up from her notes. "Everyone arrives with a definition," she says. "Almost none of them survive the first corridor."

"Ethics," she continues, tapping the page, "asks a very old pair of questions: what is good, and what is right. Not what is legal, not what is possible, not what is profitable. Whether something *should* be done at all."

She turns the notes so you can read them. "Philosophers split the work three ways. Meta-ethics asks what we even mean when we say 'good' or 'wrong', whether those words point at anything real. Normative ethics asks which general principles are correct: should we count consequences, duties, character? Applied ethics takes those principles and points them at a specific mess: medicine, business, war. AI ethics is one of the messes."

"So when I ask what AI ethics is, I am not asking you to solve meta-ethics. I am asking a narrower, more useful question: how should the people who build, sell, deploy, and operate these systems behave, so that the risks their work creates stay small? That is applied ethics. Some call it engineering ethics, and I like that name better; it puts the obligation where the hands are."

She sets down her pen. "Now, the trap. You have all read the stories: a robot with three laws bolted into its head, faithfully weighing whether to let a human come to harm. That is a real tradition, machine ethics, and it asks a genuinely different question: could the machine itself be a moral agent, capable of judging right from wrong? It is old, it is elegant, and it is almost entirely beside the point of the dungeon you are standing in."

"The systems you will meet floor after floor are not moral agents. They do not deliberate. They do not carry a conscience. Building one that could is not what stands between you and the door. What stands between you and the door is a developer who chose the training data, a manufacturer who shipped the defect, an operator who deployed the system somewhere it was never tested. Contemporary AI ethics does not require the machine to be good. It requires the people around it to be."

> AI ethics asks how the people who build, sell, and operate a system should behave, not how to give the system a conscience.

## Questions

### Which best describes what AI ethics, as it is practiced today, actually asks?

- [x] How developers, manufacturers, and operators should act to minimize the ethical risks AI creates
- [ ] Whether a machine can be taught a conscience
- [ ] Which of Asimov's laws a robot should obey first
- [ ] Whether an algorithm can be punished for a bad decision

> The other three describe machine ethics, a much older and narrower tradition that asks whether a machine could itself be a moral agent. Contemporary AI ethics is applied, or engineering, ethics aimed squarely at the humans building and deploying the system, not at the system's conscience.

### Meta-ethics, normative ethics, and applied ethics are three names for the same activity, just at different points in a career.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> They are different subfields. Meta-ethics asks what moral terms mean, normative ethics asks which principles are correct, and applied ethics asks how to act within a specific domain. AI ethics sits in the last of the three.

### A robot is programmed never to let a human come to harm through action or inaction. This design goal belongs to which tradition?

- [ ] Applied AI ethics as practiced by engineers today
- [x] Machine ethics, the older idea that a machine itself could be a moral agent
- [ ] Meta-ethics
- [ ] Legal norms for AI operators

> This is the classic machine-ethics framing: a rule built into the machine so it can weigh right and wrong for itself. It reads as AI ethics because it's about a machine and morality, but the field practiced today is concerned with the people around the machine, not with giving the machine judgement.

### Treating a self-driving car's crash-avoidance code as a small trolley-problem experiment for the car itself misunderstands where responsibility sits in AI ethics as it's practiced today.

- [x] True
- [ ] False

> The interesting ethical work happens with the designers, the manufacturer, and the operator who decided how and where to deploy the car, not with the car "solving" a moral dilemma as an agent. Framing it as the car's dilemma quietly hands the machine a moral status it doesn't have and lets the humans off the hook.
