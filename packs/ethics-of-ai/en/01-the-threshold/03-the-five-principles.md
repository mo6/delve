# The five principles

The Framework stands with five doors behind them, none open. "Name them," they say, "or stay."

"In 2019, a review counted every AI ethics guideline anyone had published: dozens of them, from governments, companies, universities. Different words, different countries. Jobin and colleagues looked for where they agreed."

"Five principles kept showing up. Not eleven. Not two. Five."

"Non-maleficence. Do no harm. First and most repeated of the five."

"Accountability, sometimes called responsibility. Someone must answer for what the system does. Not the system. Someone."

"Transparency, paired with explainability. A decision that cannot be explained cannot be checked, and cannot be trusted on faith alone."

"Justice, and fairness. The benefits and the burdens of a system should not fall on people by accident of who they are."

"Respect for human rights. Privacy chief among them. Security close behind, because a right you cannot protect is not much of a right."

"Five principles. Five questions, really, and every keeper past this door is asking one of them. Does this help without harming? Who answers for it? Can it be explained? Who does it fail? Whose rights does it touch?"

"Learn the five here. You will need every one of them further down, and I will not repeat myself twice."

> Non-maleficence, accountability, transparency, justice, and respect for human rights: the five that survived when every guideline was compared.

## Questions

### According to the convergence The Framework describes, how many principles kept reappearing across the guidelines that were compared?

- [ ] Two
- [ ] Eleven
- [x] Five
- [ ] As many as there were guidelines; no real agreement was found

> The whole point of the review was that agreement existed where you might expect none: dozens of differently worded guidelines converged on the same five principles, not on a number matching the guideline count.

### Accountability, in this framework, means the AI system itself must be able to explain and answer for its own decisions.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> Accountability attaches to a person or organisation who answers for the system, not to the system itself. That's the point of naming it separately from transparency: someone has to be answerable, and a system cannot be held to account the way a person or company can.

### A hospital's diagnostic model performs well in trials, but the vendor will not say how it reaches a diagnosis, only that "it works". Doctors cannot check its reasoning against their own judgement. Which principle is most directly failing here?

- [ ] Non-maleficence, because the model might eventually cause harm
- [x] Transparency and explainability, because the reasoning cannot be inspected or checked
- [ ] Justice and fairness, because some patients are treated differently
- [ ] Respect for human rights, because patient privacy is at risk

> As described, the specific failure is that the vendor withholds how the model reasons, which is exactly transparency and explainability. The other principles are all reasonable things to worry about with any medical AI in general, but nothing in this scenario says the model is harmful, unfair, or a privacy risk; it's opaque.

### The five principles The Framework named are meant to be checked once, at launch, and then set aside.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> They're an ongoing lens, applied through design, deployment, and continued operation, not a launch-day checklist. A system that passed the check once can still start discriminating, leaking data, or losing its explainability as it's used and updated.
