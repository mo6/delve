# Beneficence

Mira is soldering something that looks useful. "Doing good is not a slogan," she says. "It is a design constraint."

"Most of what people call AI ethics," she says without looking up, "is really risk management. Do not discriminate. Do not leak data. Do not cause the harm nobody wants their name on. Important work, all of it. But notice what it never asks: what is this system actually *for*? Whose life does it make better, and by how much?"

"That question has a name. Beneficence: the duty to do good, not merely to avoid doing harm. It is a different obligation from non-maleficence, which you will meet through that door, and it is easy to satisfy one while ignoring the other entirely. A system that helps nobody and harms nobody has cleared the bar of non-maleficence perfectly. It has done nothing for beneficence at all."

"So what counts as good, for a system built to serve people generally rather than one customer? The honest answer used in this field points at the common good, the benefit of humanity broadly, not just the shareholders who funded the model or the market it was built to capture. A diagnostic tool that only the wealthy can afford has done some good. A diagnostic tool built so anyone with the disease can be found has done more."

"Here is the part I want you to sit with, before you go further down. A researcher named Mittelstadt wrote something I have not stopped thinking about: that AI ethics is not really a special new problem. It is a microcosm, a small mirror, of the political and ethical arguments society has always had, about fairness, about who decides, about whose benefit counts. The machine did not invent these questions. It just made them urgent again, and gave them a deadline."

"You cannot build for the common good by accident. You have to ask who benefits before you ask what could go wrong, not after."

> Beneficence asks what good a system does, not merely what harm it avoids; that question has to come first, not last.

## Questions

### A system that causes no measurable harm to anyone has automatically satisfied the duty of beneficence.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> Non-maleficence and beneficence are different duties. Doing no harm clears the first; it says nothing about whether the system does any good at all. A system that helps nobody and hurts nobody has satisfied one and ignored the other.

### Mira argues that most public discussion of "AI ethics" quietly narrows to one question. Which one?

- [x] How do we minimize the harms and risks this system might cause
- [ ] How much good could this system realistically do for people
- [ ] Who owns the intellectual property behind the model
- [ ] How fast can the system be deployed

> Risk minimization dominates the public conversation, which is exactly Mira's complaint: the question of what good is actually being done gets under-asked. Ownership and deployment speed are real business questions, but they aren't what "AI ethics" discourse narrows to; the missing question is the one about benefit.

### Which best captures what Mittelstadt's observation, that AI ethics is a "microcosm" of society's challenges, means?

- [x] The disagreements AI raises about fairness and whose benefit counts are old political and ethical disagreements, now made urgent by the technology
- [ ] AI ethics is a small, self-contained field unrelated to wider politics
- [ ] AI systems have created entirely new ethical questions with no precedent
- [ ] Society's ethical disagreements will disappear once AI is regulated properly

> "Microcosm" means a small version of something larger, not something separate from it. Mittelstadt's point is that AI didn't invent these disputes about fairness and whose benefit counts; it made existing ones more urgent, which is the opposite of AI ethics being self-contained or genuinely without precedent.

### Beneficence in AI ethics, as Mira describes it, means each system should benefit its paying customer as fully as possible.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> Mira's version of beneficence points at the common good, benefit for humanity broadly, not just the customer who funded the system. A tool that serves only the people who can pay for it has done some good, but her own example, the diagnostic tool built so anyone with the disease can be found, does more precisely because it isn't limited to paying customers.
