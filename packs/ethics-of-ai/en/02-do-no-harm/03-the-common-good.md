# The common good

Commons weighs a bag of coin against an empty one. "Who pays," they ask, "and who receives?"

"Here is the deal on this floor. I will teach you how the common good gets calculated, and it will cost you something: the comfort of thinking it's simple arithmetic."

"The idea itself is old. Utilitarianism, consequentialism if you want the family name: judge an action by its outcomes, and choose whichever spreads the most wellbeing across everyone affected. For an AI system deployed at scale, that is tempting. Add up every benefit, subtract every harm, ship whatever nets positive. Clean ledger."

"Except the ledger lies about how clean it is. First: you cannot actually measure wellbeing the way you measure coin. A convenience gained by ten thousand people and a privacy lost by one are not the same currency, and putting them on the same spreadsheet is a choice dressed up as arithmetic."

"Second: a benefit does not count the same twice over. Give one person a second convenience and a hundredth person their first, and the hundredth gains more, even if the numbers on the invoice look identical. Aggregate totals hide exactly whose need was already met and whose was not."

"Third, and this is the one that should worry you most: rights do not show up as a number you can outweigh with enough small benefits elsewhere. A right to privacy, a right not to know something about yourself, a right to consent, these can be traded against 'the greater good' on a spreadsheet and lose every time, because a million small conveniences will always outvote one person's veto if all you are doing is adding. Philosophers have a monster for this: imagine someone whose wellbeing scales without limit, feeding on everyone else's small losses, and the utilitarian math says feed it. Something has gone wrong before you ever reach that conclusion."

"So spend carefully. The common good is a real question worth asking, whose benefit, whose cost. It stops being a good question the moment you let one number decide it alone. Bring more than one viewpoint to the ledger, or I am not opening this door."

> Aggregate wellbeing is a real question, not a real number; a right does not disappear just because enough small benefits outvote it.

## Questions

### Maximizing aggregate wellbeing is a straightforward calculation once you have good data, since benefits and harms can simply be added and subtracted like coins.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> This is the incommensurability problem: good data doesn't fix the deeper issue that different kinds of benefit and harm, a convenience versus a privacy loss, aren't the same currency. Putting them on one spreadsheet is a choice, not a neutral calculation.

### A company argues that a surveillance feature is justified because it gives a small convenience benefit to ten million users, which outweighs a serious privacy loss for the one thousand users who are wrongly flagged. What is the strongest problem with this reasoning, as Commons frames it?

- [x] It treats a right, like privacy, as just another quantity that enough small benefits elsewhere can outweigh
- [ ] Ten million is not actually a large enough number to justify any harm
- [ ] Convenience should never be counted as a real benefit
- [ ] The company has miscounted; it should be exactly one million users, not ten thousand

> The problem isn't the specific numbers, it's the category error: treating a right as tradeable against enough small conveniences elsewhere, when a right is exactly the kind of thing aggregate math shouldn't be allowed to outvote.

### The "utility monster" thought experiment is used to illustrate what problem with pure aggregate-wellbeing reasoning?

- [ ] That wellbeing can never be measured at all, in any circumstance
- [x] That if you only add up benefits, a hypothetical being who gains disproportionately could justify taking from everyone else without limit
- [ ] That monsters are a common feature of AI systems deployed at scale
- [ ] That utilitarianism was invented specifically to justify AI surveillance

> The thought experiment imagines a being whose wellbeing scales without limit, so pure addition says feed it at everyone else's expense. That conclusion is absurd, which is the point: something is wrong with reasoning that only adds, not that measurement itself is impossible.

### Because measuring collective wellbeing is genuinely hard, the honest response is to consult a single expert metric rather than gather diverse viewpoints.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> Commons argues the opposite: bring more than one viewpoint to the ledger. Replacing the difficulty with a single metric, expert-chosen or not, recreates the exact problem the lesson describes, one number standing in for a question that isn't one number's to answer.
