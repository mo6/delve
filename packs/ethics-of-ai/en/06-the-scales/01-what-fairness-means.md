# What fairness means

Justine adjusts the scales and they still disagree. "Fairness is not one number," she says. "Anyone who sells you one is lying."

She has two pairs of scales on her bench, calibrated differently on purpose. "This first
pair says fairness means everyone gets the same probability of the benefit, regardless of
anything else about them. Sounds right, doesn't it? Now this second pair. It says fairness
means *recognising* real differences between people and treating them accordingly, rather
than pretending everyone starts from the same place. Also sounds right. They disagree with
each other constantly."

That disagreement isn't a bug in the theory; it's the actual content of the debate. Treating
everyone identically can entrench an existing disadvantage, because identical treatment of
people who started unequal just preserves the gap. Treating people according to their
circumstances can look, to someone else, exactly like favouritism.

"Why does this matter beyond philosophy seminars?" She doesn't wait for you to answer.
"Because a society people believe is fair holds together, and one they believe is rigged
doesn't. That's not my opinion, that's Rawls: fairness is a condition for social cooperation
to keep functioning at all. And separately, treating someone unfairly is a direct insult to
their standing as a person who matters equally, which is Kant's half of the argument. Two
different philosophers, two different routes, same conclusion: this isn't decoration."

Justine sets a real example on the bench. "Say a country builds a national exam-grading
algorithm and discovers it consistently gives certain schools' students higher marks than a
human grader would have. Someone spots a fix: adjust for each school's historic average, so
no school's grades drift from its own baseline." She waits. "Sounds like a fair correction.
Except a school's historic average is itself shaped by decades of which neighbourhoods got
funding, which families could afford tutoring. Correct the algorithm to match that history
and you've built the socioeconomic pattern permanently into the grade, just with better
arithmetic behind it."

That's the trap: a fix aimed at matching a historical baseline actively worked against a
different, equally reasonable goal, giving individual students a mark that reflected their
own work. Neither metric is wrong to want. You cannot maximise both at once, and pretending
otherwise is exactly the lie she opened with.

> Fairness is not a single number you optimise for; it's a genuine conflict between reasonable definitions, and picking one always costs you something on another.

## Questions

### Once you choose a specific fairness metric to optimise, such as equal probability of a benefit across groups, you have solved fairness for that system.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> Choosing one reasonable definition of fairness means you have, by construction, given up
> ground on the other reasonable definitions it conflicts with. There's no metric that
> satisfies both "treat everyone the same" and "recognise real differences" at once, so
> picking one is a trade-off, not a solution.

### A national exam-grading algorithm is adjusted so every school's grades match that school's historic average. What problem does Justine identify with this fix?

- [x] The historic average itself reflects decades of unequal funding and opportunity, so matching it locks that inequality into individual grades
- [ ] The adjustment makes the algorithm too slow to run at scale
- [ ] Historic averages cannot be calculated accurately from past records
- [ ] The fix removes the algorithm's ability to grade at all

> Speed and calculability aren't the objection Justine raises, and the algorithm still
> grades fine under this fix; it just grades toward the wrong target. The historic average
> is not a neutral baseline, it's a record of past inequality, so matching it preserves that
> inequality with cleaner-looking numbers.

### Justine describes two philosophers who argue, from different directions, that fairness matters beyond personal preference. What are the two grounds she cites?

- [x] Fairness as a condition for social cooperation to hold together (Rawls), and fairness as respect for a person's equal standing (Kant)
- [ ] Fairness as a legal requirement in most jurisdictions, and fairness as a marketing advantage
- [ ] Fairness as something only relevant to grading algorithms specifically
- [ ] Fairness as whatever a majority vote decides in the moment

> Justine names Rawls and Kant specifically, not legal codes or marketing, and neither
> argument is scoped to grading algorithms alone; the example is just where she applies it.
> A majority vote is exactly the "one number" framing she opens the lesson rejecting.

### Treating two people identically, regardless of the different circumstances they started from, is always the fairest option.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> Identical treatment of people who started from unequal positions preserves that inequality
> rather than correcting for it. It's one reasonable notion of fairness, not automatically
> the fairest one; the competing notion, recognising real differences, is just as defensible
> and sometimes points the opposite way.
