# Rights and AI

Charter unfolds a long parchment. "Principles are useful," they say. "Rights are enforceable. Know the difference."

"Every ethics document you'll read talks about values," Charter says, weighting down the
corners of the parchment with stones. "Fairness, transparency, dignity. Good words, all of
them, and none you can take to court. A right is different: it's a claim that somebody,
somewhere, is obliged to honour, whether they feel like it or not."

That's why the serious guidelines on AI keep circling back to human rights instead of
inventing new vocabulary. Rights already have what ethics slogans lack: they're
**universal** (they don't depend on which country wrote your passport), and in most legal
systems they're **enforceable** (there is somewhere to complain, and someone obliged to
answer).

Charter traces a finger along the parchment. "Two things a rights foundation actually asks of
a system. First: protect people's **agency**, their capacity to decide their own life, rather
than deciding it for them under the excuse of convenience. Second: when a right and something
else collide, wealth, efficiency, national competitiveness, the right wins. That's not a nice
sentiment, it's the whole reason we call it a right instead of a preference."

"Now the nuance, because someone always asks." They hold up a hand before you can. "Rights
collide with *each other*, too, and not every collision resolves the same way. A right to
life outweighs a right to privacy when the two are genuinely in conflict; nobody seriously
disputes that. Most collisions aren't that clean, which is exactly why you need judgement
and not a lookup table."

What this cashes out to, in practice: don't build or deploy a system that plainly violates
someone's rights, and don't build one that quietly blocks people from *enjoying* rights they
technically still hold. A hiring algorithm that never violates anyone's stated legal right
but reliably keeps disabled applicants from ever reaching an interview has failed the same
test through the back door.

> A right isn't a value you'd like respected; it's a claim someone is obliged to honour, and that's what a value alone can never be.

## Questions

### "Fairness" and "the right to non-discrimination" are the same kind of claim, just worded differently.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> Fairness is a value: something worth wanting, with nobody specifically obliged to deliver
> it. A right to non-discrimination is a claim that someone is obliged to honour, and that a
> complaint can be brought against if they don't. The two can point at similar outcomes
> without being the same kind of thing.

### According to Charter, what does a genuine right have that a value like "fairness" or "transparency" typically lacks?

- [x] Enforceability: someone is obliged to honour it, and there's somewhere to hold them to that
- [ ] Popularity: more people agree that it matters
- [ ] Precision: it can always be reduced to a single number
- [ ] Age: rights have existed for longer than corporate ethics guidelines

> Charter never claims rights are more popular, more precise, or older than the alternative;
> plenty of values are ancient and widely shared too. The distinction they draw is
> specifically about obligation: a right has somewhere to go when it's broken.

### A system that never explicitly violates a stated right, but reliably blocks a group from enjoying rights they hold, still fails a rights-based ethical test.

- [x] True
- [ ] False

> Charter's hiring-algorithm example makes exactly this point: technical compliance with
> every named right isn't the same as respecting them, if the practical effect is that one
> group can never actually exercise them.

### When two genuine rights conflict, for example the right to life against the right to privacy, what does Charter say generally happens?

- [x] The graver right, like the right to life, generally outweighs the lesser one, though most conflicts are less clear-cut
- [ ] Whichever right is easier to measure automatically wins
- [ ] A rights conflict means neither right actually applies
- [ ] The most recently written law always decides the conflict

> Charter is explicit that this particular pairing, life against privacy, is one of the rare
> clean cases. They're just as explicit that most collisions between rights don't resolve
> that neatly, which is why judgement matters and a lookup table wouldn't.
