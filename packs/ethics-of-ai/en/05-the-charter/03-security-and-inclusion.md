# Security and inclusion

Threshold Rights counts who is already inside. "A system that works for most," they say, "and fails for some, has already chosen."

Their stall sits right at the doorway, and they check everyone who passes, not to keep people
out, but to see who almost didn't get counted at all.

"Security, first, because people mean the wrong thing by it." They tap a ledger. "It's not
firewalls. In this context, security is the right to be protected from harm that an AI
system enables: a facial recognition system misused to track someone without cause, a
scoring model that lets an abuser locate a victim, a voice clone built from a few seconds of
audio and used to defraud someone's family. The system doesn't have to be malicious to cause
this; it just has to be usable by someone who is."

"Then inclusion, which is where most rooms go quiet." Threshold Rights leans on the counter.
"A voice assistant trained mostly on one accent works brilliantly, for most people. A
symptom-checker trained mostly on one skin tone performs well, for most people. Notice the
shape of that sentence. 'Works for most' sounds like success until you ask who the remainder
is, and the remainder is never random. It's disabled people, older people, people who don't
speak the training language natively, people whose faces or voices or bodies weren't in the
room when the dataset was built."

"Here's the part I actually charge for." They hold up a coin. "Building a system and only
later noticing who it excludes is not a neutral accident you can apologise for after the
fact. The exclusion was a decision, made the moment nobody checked, whether or not anyone
meant to make it. 'We didn't think about it' has the same effects as 'we chose not to
include them.'"

That's what ties inclusion to **dignity**: being systematically the exception, the case the
system wasn't built for, the person who has to phone a human because the automated system
can't parse them, is its own kind of harm, independent of whatever the system's overall
accuracy number says.

> A system that fails for the same people every time hasn't found an edge case; it's made a choice about who counts.

## Questions

### If an AI system performs well for the large majority of users, it has met a reasonable bar for inclusion.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> "Works for most" is exactly the phrase Threshold Rights warns about. The remainder a
> system fails is never random; it tends to be the same groups every time, which is a
> pattern of exclusion, not a rounding error the majority figure can excuse.

### Which of these is the clearest example of a security risk in Threshold Rights's sense of the word?

- [x] A voice-cloning tool built from a few seconds of audio being used to defraud someone's family
- [ ] A facial recognition system correctly identifying an authorised employee at a building entrance
- [ ] A scoring model producing a slightly lower accuracy score than last year's version
- [ ] A recommendation system suggesting a product a customer doesn't want

> Correct identification at a door and an unwanted product suggestion are the systems
> working as intended, and a small accuracy dip is a performance issue, not a rights one.
> Security in this sense is about harm the system *enables* for someone else to inflict,
> exactly what the voice-cloning fraud example is.

### A symptom-checker app performs accurately for most users but consistently underperforms for one skin tone underrepresented in its training data. How does Threshold Rights say this exclusion should be understood?

- [x] As a decision, made the moment nobody checked who was included, not a neutral accident
- [ ] As an unavoidable limitation of all statistical models
- [ ] As a problem only worth fixing once it affects the majority of users
- [ ] As a marketing issue rather than an ethical one

> Calling it unavoidable or a marketing concern is exactly the framing Threshold Rights
> rejects, and waiting until it affects a majority defeats the point: the harm lands on the
> same excluded group either way, and it was a choice, however unintentional, not a law of
> statistics.

### Being the person a system consistently fails to serve, so that you have to fall back on a human every time, causes no harm as long as the fallback option exists.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> The existence of a fallback doesn't erase the harm of being, every time, the exception the
> system wasn't built for. That repeated experience is itself a dignity harm, separate from
> whether an alternative eventually gets you served.
