# Where ethics goes next

The Open Door stands aside. "The scroll is not the end," they say. "It is permission to keep asking."

They don't block your way, they never have. "Before you take the scroll, one honest
conversation, because every keeper before me taught you a principle and I'd rather leave you
with a habit."

"First: ask who benefits. Who benefits from a company publishing an ethics framework? Often,
genuinely, the people the system affects. Sometimes, just as genuinely, the company's own
reputation, at a fraction of the cost of actually changing the system. Both can be true of
the same document. Learning to tell which one you're reading is worth more than memorising
another list of principles."

"Second: ask who the framework doesn't mention. Every AI ethics conversation of the last
decade has mostly been conducted by people in a handful of countries, about systems mostly
deployed on everyone else. Whose harms get a paragraph, and whose don't get asked about at
all, tells you as much as whatever the document actually says."

"Third, and people forget this one: not everyone building AI is trying to do the right thing
badly. Some are trying to do harm well. A framework written only for well-intentioned
developers has a blind spot exactly the size of everyone who wasn't listening."

The Open Door gestures at the corridor behind them, empty, leading nowhere in particular.
"There's an old joke among engineers, fitting a bicycle's brake onto an airplane and calling
the plane safer. That's what a principles document does if nobody ever checks whether it
changed a single decision: motion that looks like safety and stops nothing, because it was
never connected to anything that could actually apply the brake."

"So here's what I actually want you to leave with." They step aside fully now. "Not a belief
that you've finished, that you've learned the five rooms and can stop asking. The scroll
behind me isn't a certificate that says you're done. It's the thing that lets you walk back
out into rooms nobody built a keeper for yet, and ask the same questions anyway."

> Finishing this dungeon doesn't mean you've learned the answers; it means you've learned which questions to keep asking after the keepers stop.

## Questions

### Asking "who benefits from this ethics framework" is a cynical distraction from the actual content of the framework.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> The Open Door isn't dismissing the framework's content by asking this; they're pointing
> out that a document can genuinely help the people it affects and genuinely serve the
> publisher's reputation at the same time. Asking cui bono is how you tell which parts are
> which, not a reason to ignore the document.

### The Open Door's bicycle-brake-on-an-airplane image is used to illustrate what?

- [x] An ethics document that exists but is never connected to any decision it could actually change
- [ ] The idea that safety features from one industry never transfer to another
- [ ] A recommendation that AI systems should have physical safety mechanisms
- [ ] The claim that airplanes are inherently safer than bicycles

> The image isn't about literal safety hardware or comparing vehicles. It's about a brake
> fitted to something it was never wired into: motion that looks like a safety measure while
> doing nothing, which is exactly what a principles document is if it never touches an
> actual decision.

### Most public conversation about AI ethics over the last decade has been conducted by, and mostly about, a fairly narrow set of countries and contexts.

- [x] True
- [ ] False

> This is the second question The Open Door presses: whose harms get discussed, and whose
> don't, is itself information, and the conversation has been geographically narrow relative
> to where AI systems actually get deployed.

### Why does The Open Door insist that not everyone building AI is trying to do the right thing, even badly?

- [x] Because a framework written only for well-meaning developers leaves a blind spot for anyone deliberately trying to cause harm
- [ ] Because most AI developers are known to act in bad faith
- [ ] Because ethics frameworks are only relevant to malicious actors
- [ ] Because good intentions always guarantee good outcomes

> The Open Door doesn't claim most developers act in bad faith, and frameworks are still
> useful to well-meaning ones. The point is narrower: a framework that only imagines a
> well-intentioned reader has nothing to say about the minority who aren't, and that gap is
> exactly where harm gets through.
