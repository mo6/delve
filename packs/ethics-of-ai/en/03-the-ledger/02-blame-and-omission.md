# Blame and omission

The Omission says nothing for a long moment. Then: "What you did not do still counts. Not always. Often enough."

"An act is something you did. An omission is something you did not do, when you could have. Ordinary moral instinct treats these differently, and it is not wrong to: pushing someone into a river is usually judged worse than watching someone drown when you could have thrown a rope. That gap is real, and this floor is not here to erase it."

"But the gap is not a wall. An omission becomes blameworthy the moment it is deliberate and knowing: you saw the risk, you had the ability to act, and you chose not to. A city official who receives a report that a water system is contaminated, and sits on it rather than warning anyone, has not thrown poison into a well. They have still done something a court would recognize, and give a name to: negligence. The harm arrived through an absence, not an action, and the absence was still a choice."

"Here is the part that trips people up in the other direction. You cannot be held responsible for every good thing you failed to do. You did not save every stranger you could theoretically have helped today; nobody could, and treating every missed opportunity as a moral failure makes the word 'responsible' useless by stretching it over everything. The line is not 'could you have acted' in the abstract. It is closer to: did you know of a specific, foreseeable risk, did you have a specific ability to reduce it, and did you decide not to anyway."

"Bring that to the systems you are examining. A company that ships a model, and later receives credible reports that it discriminates, and changes nothing for eighteen months, is not merely slow. That silence, once the report lands on a desk, is close kin to the act that caused the original harm. It is not identical. It is not nothing."

> An omission is not automatically innocent; once you know the risk and have the power to act, choosing not to is a choice, and it can be blamed like one.

## Questions

### Deliberately choosing not to act, once you know about a specific harm and have the power to prevent it, can be morally blameworthy in a way similar to causing that harm directly.

- [x] True
- [ ] False

> This is The Omission's central claim, illustrated by the city official who knows of the contamination and sits on the report; the harm arrives through an absence, but the absence was still a choice, and a knowing one.

### A company receives a credible report that its deployed AI system discriminates against a group of users, and makes no changes for eighteen months. Which of the following best captures why this counts as more than "merely slow"?

- [x] The company had specific knowledge of the risk and the ability to act, and chose not to; that combination is what turns an omission into something blameworthy
- [ ] Any delay of more than a year is automatically illegal
- [ ] Omissions are always judged exactly as harshly as direct acts
- [ ] The system caused the harm, so the company's response afterward is irrelevant

> The key factors are specific knowledge, specific ability, and a choice not to act, not the raw length of the delay. The Omission is also careful to say the omission is "close kin" to the original act, not identical to it, so treating omissions and acts as automatically equal overstates the claim.

### Because every omission is potentially blameworthy once harm results, a person is morally responsible for every good outcome they failed to bring about.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> This stretches responsibility over everything, which is exactly what The Omission warns against. The actual test is a specific foreseeable risk plus a specific ability to reduce it plus a choice not to, not a general duty to have prevented every harm anywhere.

### Which pair correctly matches an act and an omission that could both ground moral responsibility, according to The Omission's reasoning?

- [x] A developer who deliberately hides a known safety flaw (act), and a manager who receives a credible bug report about that flaw and files it away unread by choice (omission)
- [ ] A developer who writes code (act), and a user who never learns to code (omission)
- [ ] A company launching a product (act), and a competitor choosing not to launch a rival product (omission)
- [ ] A system generating an output (act), and a system failing to generate any output at all (omission)

> The correct pair involves a deliberate, knowing choice tied to a specific foreseeable risk on both sides. The other omissions involve no known risk and no duty to act, learning to code, launching a competing product, or a system simply producing no output, so none of them are the kind of omission that grounds blame.
