# Moral agency

Agentia closes a thick book. "An algorithm does not have a conscience," she says. "Stop pretending it does."

"A moral agent is a very specific thing. Not anything that acts. Not anything that decides. A moral agent is something that can act *with reference to* right and wrong: weigh an action against a sense of what it ought to do, and be capable of choosing otherwise. That capacity is what moral responsibility actually rests on. You cannot be blamed, in any meaningful sense, for something you had no way to evaluate and no way to have done differently."

"Think of an animal. A dog that knocks over a vase is not evil. It has no access to the concept. We do not put dogs on trial, because whatever a dog is doing, it is not weighing right against wrong and choosing wrong anyway. We might still be angry, or grieve the vase, but the word 'blame' does not really fit, and anyone who has owned a dog already knows this without being taught it."

"Now: your AI system. It can classify, predict, generate, act in the world with real consequences. What it cannot do, not the systems built so far, is hold a concept of 'ought' and choose against it. It optimizes an objective function. That is not the same act, however sophisticated the output looks from outside. Autonomy in the philosophical sense, and the capacity to actually evaluate consequences as consequences that matter, both are missing. Without them, moral responsibility has nowhere to attach on the machine's side of the room."

"This is not a comforting conclusion for people who would rather blame the algorithm and go home. If the system is not a moral agent, the responsibility did not evaporate. It moved. It sits with whoever trained it, whoever deployed it, whoever kept using it after the first warning sign. Accountability does not require a moral agent behind the harm. It requires one somewhere in the chain that produced it, and that somewhere is never the model itself."

> A system that cannot weigh right against wrong cannot be blamed for either; the responsibility does not vanish, it moves to the people around it.

## Questions

### What does Agentia say is required for something to count as a moral agent?

- [x] The capacity to weigh an action against a sense of right and wrong, and to have chosen otherwise
- [ ] The capacity to produce outputs that affect the real world
- [ ] The capacity to process large amounts of data quickly
- [ ] Being built by a human rather than occurring naturally

> Real-world effect, processing speed, and human origin are all beside the point Agentia is making. The specific capacity moral agency requires is weighing right against wrong and being able to have chosen differently, which is exactly what current AI systems lack.

### A dog that damages property is not blamed the way a person would be, because a dog has no access to the concept of right and wrong it is choosing against.

- [ ] False

> This is Agentia's own example: we might be angry or grieve the loss, but "blame" doesn't really fit an animal that has no way to weigh right against wrong in the first place.

### Because current AI systems are not moral agents, no one can be held morally responsible when they cause harm.

- [ ] True
- [x] False

> The opposite is Agentia's point. Responsibility doesn't disappear when the system isn't a moral agent, it moves to the humans and organisations who trained, deployed, or kept using it.
